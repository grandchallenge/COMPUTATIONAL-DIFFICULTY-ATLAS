## Question: can a deliberate coin flip make an algorithm more useful?

Some algorithms make random choices yet guarantee the correct answer; they vary only in how long they take. Others always stop quickly but allow a small probability of error. These two kinds of randomized computation have very different contracts.

Randomness is not a substitute for a definition of correctness. A scientific claim about a probabilistic algorithm must specify *what is random*, *which inputs are covered*, *how long computation may take*, and *how likely each answer is to be wrong*.

This chapter introduces RP, coRP, ZPP and BPP, explains amplification, and gives a concrete algebraic example where one incorrect answer is impossible.

## 1. Input versus random coins

Fix a binary input $x$ of length $n$. A randomized machine $M$ can be represented as a deterministic machine receiving both $x$ and a string $r$ of independent uniformly random bits. For fixed $x,r$, its behavior and answer are completely determined.

The probability of an answer is computed **over $r$**, while the input $x$ remains fixed. A worst-case randomized running-time claim must say whether it bounds all random choices, expected time over the coins, or a tail probability.

We use worst-case polynomial time **on every input and every coin sequence** for BPP, RP and coRP decision machines. ZPP will have zero error with polynomial **expected** running time.

An average over a distribution of problem instances is a different measure. An algorithm that succeeds on most inputs from one distribution need not satisfy the BPP definition, which requires its success guarantee for **every** input.

## 2. BPP: bounded two-sided error

A language $L$ is in BPP if some randomized polynomial-time decider $M$ has, for every encoded input $x$,

$$
x\in L\Longrightarrow
\Pr_r[M(x;r)=1]\ge\frac23,
$$

and

$$
x\notin L\Longrightarrow
\Pr_r[M(x;r)=1]\le\frac13.
$$

Both kinds of mistake are allowed, but each is bounded by $1/3$. The constants are conventional: any fixed success advantage bounded away from $1/2$ can be amplified to another constant by polynomially many independent repetitions.

**Example of the distinction.** A probabilistic classifier that predicts the right answer on 99% of samples from a chosen distribution has **not** thereby been shown to decide a language in BPP. Its guarantee could fail on rare inputs. A BPP theorem must hold on all binary inputs under the specified total-language convention.

**Basic containment.** $P\subseteq BPP$: a deterministic polynomial-time machine simply ignores the random coins and has zero error.

## 3. Amplification: repeated independent trials

Suppose $M$ succeeds with probability at least $2/3$ on every input. Run it $k$ times with **independent** random-bit strings and take the majority answer. With $k$ odd, there is no tie.

The event that the majority is wrong requires at least $(k+1)/2$ erroneous runs. A Chernoff or Hoeffding bound gives

$$
\Pr[\text{majority is wrong}]
\le \exp\left(-\frac{k}{18}\right),
$$

using the initial error upper bound $1/3$ (this numerical exponent is a deliberately conservative standard bound). Taking $k=O(\log(1/\delta))$ reduces the error to at most $\delta$ while multiplying runtime by only $k$.

**Independence matters.** Repeating the **same** random seed may repeat the same error every time; amplification depends on independent trials or a rigorously justified alternative source of randomness.

Amplification reduces a nonzero error probability but does not turn a bounded-error Monte Carlo procedure into a zero-error algorithm.

## 4. RP and coRP: errors allowed on only one side

A language lies in **RP** if some randomized polynomial-time decider has

$$
x\notin L\Longrightarrow\Pr[M(x;r)=1]=0,
$$

and

$$
x\in L\Longrightarrow\Pr[M(x;r)=1]\ge\frac12.
$$

Thus **YES is trustworthy**: an RP machine never accepts a no-instance. It may fail to discover acceptance on some yes-instances.

A language lies in **coRP** if it is the complement of an RP language. Equivalently, one can require

$$
x\in L\Longrightarrow\Pr[M(x;r)=1]=1,
$$

and

$$
x\notin L\Longrightarrow\Pr[M(x;r)=0]\ge\frac12.
$$

Thus **NO is trustworthy**: a coRP machine never rejects a yes-instance.

The direction is easy to reverse by mistake. RP is *one-sided false-negative* for its target YES language; coRP is *one-sided false-positive*.

Because a one-sided error bound of $1/2$ can be reduced by independent repetition with an appropriate OR/AND rule, both RP and coRP are contained in BPP.

## 5. A concrete example: detecting a nonzero polynomial

Let $f(t)$ be a univariate polynomial of degree at most $d$ over a field $F$. A nonzero polynomial of degree at most $d$ has at most $d$ distinct roots in that field.

Choose uniformly at random an element $a$ from a finite subset $S\subseteq F$ with $|S|\ge2d$ (and with $S$ nonempty), and evaluate $f(a)$.

- If $f$ is the zero polynomial, **every** evaluation returns zero.
- If $f$ is nonzero, at most $d$ choices make $f(a)=0$, so

$$
\Pr_{a\in S}[f(a)\ne0]\ge1-\frac{d}{|S|}\ge\frac12.
$$

The algorithm answering YES when it finds a nonzero evaluation is an **RP** procedure for the language NONZERO-POLY. Equivalently, answering "identically zero" unless a nonzero value is found gives a **coRP** procedure for zero-polynomial identity testing under this explicit representation.

For polynomial-time complexity, the field arithmetic, sampling from $S$, evaluation, and the degree/encoding bounds must themselves admit polynomial-bit-cost implementation. The simple example assumes explicit univariate coefficients and a sufficiently large efficiently represented field or evaluation set. General arithmetic-circuit identity testing needs additional encoding/degree details and cannot be classified by this toy argument alone.

**Worked finite example.** Over $\mathbb F_7$, take

$$
f(t)=t(t-1),\qquad S=\{0,1,2,3\}.
$$

The polynomial is zero at $0$ and $1$ but nonzero at $2$ and $3$. One random trial finds a nonzero value with probability exactly $1/2$; a false YES for the zero-polynomial test cannot occur when a nonzero evaluation is seen. Independent repetition can increase the probability of detecting that this specific $f$ is nonzero.

The root bound generalizes through the Schwartz–Zippel lemma; the present one-variable case follows directly from elementary algebra. This is a concrete algorithmic *upper bound*, not a proof that polynomial identity testing lies outside P.

## 6. ZPP: zero error, expected polynomial time

In a **Las Vegas algorithm**, answers are always correct, but running time depends on random choices. In contrast, a **Monte Carlo algorithm** has a bounded running time and may return an incorrect answer with small probability.

The class **ZPP** contains languages decidable by zero-error randomized algorithms whose expected running time is polynomial in the input length for every input. An equivalent standard characterization is

$$
ZPP=RP\cap coRP.
$$

**One direction, constructively.** Suppose $L\in RP\cap coRP$. Run an RP algorithm $A$ and a coRP algorithm $B$ on the same input with independent fresh coins. If $A$ outputs YES, accept: that outcome is never wrong. If $B$ outputs NO, reject: that outcome is never wrong. Otherwise try again.

On a yes-instance, $A$ returns a certifying YES with probability at least $1/2$ each round; on a no-instance, $B$ returns a certifying NO with probability at least $1/2$. The expected number of rounds is at most $2$. Each round takes polynomial time, so the expected total time is polynomial and the output never errs.

**Converse idea.** A zero-error expected-polynomial-time algorithm can be truncated after a sufficiently large multiple of its expected runtime. On timeout, answering NO yields an RP algorithm, and answering YES yields a coRP algorithm. Markov's inequality bounds timeout probability; with an appropriately chosen polynomial cutoff, each machine succeeds with probability at least $1/2$ on its permitted-error side.

This equivalence relates the *two one-sided certificate directions* to a never-wrong procedure with variable duration. It does not imply that the intersection equals P.

## 7. BPP versus PP and PSPACE

C13 introduced PP using a strict majority threshold, with no fixed probability gap away from $1/2$. The bounded error of BPP immediately implies

$$
BPP\subseteq PP,
$$

but a general PP machine may accept with probability only slightly above one half on yes-instances. Polynomially many repetitions of such a tiny advantage need not produce BPP-type reliability.

BPP also lies in PSPACE: if a machine uses polynomially many random bits, a deterministic space-bounded simulation can enumerate all seeds, evaluate each computation and count accepting outcomes with a polynomial-bit counter. Such enumeration may take exponential time, but it reuses polynomial working space.

Thus

$$
P\subseteq ZPP\subseteq RP\subseteq BPP\subseteq PP\subseteq PSPACE
$$

and symmetrically $ZPP\subseteq coRP\subseteq BPP$.

**Unknown boundaries.** We do not know whether $P=BPP$ in the unrelativized standard model. Cryptographic and circuit-hardness assumptions can imply derandomization results under specified hypotheses, but these are not unconditional proofs that every randomized polynomial-time algorithm can be replaced by a deterministic one with polynomial worst-case time.

## 8. Why random guessing does not put SAT in BPP

A tempting proposal for SAT is to choose a uniformly random assignment and evaluate the formula. For a satisfiable formula with a **unique** satisfying assignment among $m$ variables, one guess succeeds with probability $2^{-m}$. Polynomially many independent guesses generally do not amplify this exponentially tiny probability into a constant.

The algorithm does have one-sided **soundness**: if it finds a satisfying assignment, the formula is satisfiable. But without a guaranteed constant success probability on every satisfiable input, it does **not** meet RP's definition for SAT, let alone establish SAT in BPP.

This is the same lesson as C04 and C08: quickly checking a certificate does not give an efficient method for finding one with adequate success probability.

## 9. Common mistakes

- **“Randomization means average-case complexity.”** Random coins are averaged with the input fixed; average-case analysis averages over a specified input distribution.
- **“A 99% success rate on a test set proves BPP membership.”** Worst-case per-input guarantees are required.
- **“RP may incorrectly accept a no-instance.”** Its one-sided error is in the opposite direction.
- **“Amplification makes every random algorithm exact.”** It reduces errors when an initial constant advantage and independence are present; it does not remove all error.
- **“PP is BPP with a different name.”** PP permits an arbitrarily small positive majority advantage.
- **“A Las Vegas algorithm always has a fixed polynomial cutoff.”** The standard ZPP definition guarantees expected, not necessarily worst-case, polynomial time.
- **“Randomly guessing SAT witnesses settles P versus NP.”** Some satisfying formulas have exponentially rare witnesses.
