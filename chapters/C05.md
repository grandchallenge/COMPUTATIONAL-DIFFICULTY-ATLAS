# C05 — Reductions: Translating Problems, Transporting Difficulty

**Status:** Reviewed editorial candidate; corrections from CDA-REV-C05-002 applied; publication acceptance pending.  
**Prerequisites:** C01 on decision languages, C04 on verification and NP.

## Question: if B were easy, would A become easy?

Imagine owning a reliable machine for solving one very particular puzzle. You are faced with another puzzle, but you know how to convert every input to the first kind so that yes and no answers stay unchanged. You can then solve the new puzzle by translating it, operating the machine, and reading the answer.

This is the idea of a **reduction**. It is not merely a resemblance between topics. It is an algorithm whose direction and resource cost are specified. A reduction from A to B says a **B-solver could solve A**, not the other way around.

## Section 1 — Polynomial-time many-one reductions

Let $A,B\subseteq\{0,1\}^*$ be decision languages. We write $A\le_m^P B$ if there exists a total deterministic polynomial-time computable function $f:\{0,1\}^*\to\{0,1\}^*$ such that

$$
\forall x,\quad x\in A\iff f(x)\in B.
$$

The map $f$ is called a polynomial-time many-one (or Karp) reduction. Its requirements are unusually strict: it transforms any input in polynomial time, makes a **single** target instance, and preserves **both** positive and negative answers. If a translator handles yes-instances but maps some no-instances to yes-instances, it fails the reduction definition.

**Analogy.** A translator that changes “yes” into “no” is not translating the question, even if its output looks superficially similar.

## Section 2 — The algorithm-transport theorem

**Theorem.** If $A\le_m^P B$ and $B\in P$, then $A\in P$.

**Proof.** Let $f$ be the reduction and $D_B$ a polynomial-time decider for B. Given $x$, calculate $f(x)$, then run $D_B(f(x))$. If $f$ requires at most $O(n^a)$ steps, its output length is at most $O(n^a)$; if $D_B$ takes $O(m^b)$ on length-$m$ input, the composite runs in $O(n^a+n^{ab})$, a polynomial. Its answer is correct by the membership equivalence. $\square$

**Corollary (contrapositive).** If $A\le_m^P B$ and $A\notin P$, then $B\notin P$. This implication is valid, but proving a useful premise $A\notin P$ is itself often difficult.

Notice what we **cannot** infer: an efficient solver for A says nothing about B from $A\le_m^P B$ alone. The arrow of reduction points toward the potentially more powerful solver.

## Section 3 — A worked transformation: undirected to directed reachability

An **undirected reachability instance** consists of a finite graph $G=(V,E)$ and vertices $s,t$, and asks if a path from $s$ to $t$ exists. The **directed** problem uses an oriented graph.

Construct $G'=(V,E')$ by replacing each edge $\{u,v\}\in E$ with both arcs $(u,v)$ and $(v,u)$. Leave $s,t$ unchanged. Every undirected path maps to a directed walk in $G'$, so reachability in $G$ implies reachability in $G'$. Conversely every arc of $G'$ arose from an undirected edge of $G$; forgetting its orientation converts a directed path back into an undirected walk, which implies reachability. Hence answers agree.

The transformation can be produced by scanning each encoded edge once and emitting at most twice as many arcs, together with vertex and endpoint metadata; it is polynomial in the encoded input size. Invalid encodings can be handled by a fixed deterministic convention. Thus undirected reachability many-one reduces to directed reachability.

**Toy instance.** With undirected edges $\{a,b\},\{b,c\}$, asking whether a reaches c is a yes-instance. After translation the arcs $a\to b$ and $b\to c$ exist. Delete $\{b,c\}$, and both the original and transformed instances become no-instances for reachability from a to c. A sound reduction handles both outcomes.

## Section 4 — Composition of reductions

**Theorem.** If $A\le_m^P B$ via $f$ and $B\le_m^P C$ via $g$, then $A\le_m^P C$ via $g\circ f$.

**Proof.** For any input $x$,

$$
x\in A\iff f(x)\in B\iff g(f(x))\in C.
$$

Polynomial-time computable functions produce polynomial-length outputs, so composing two such functions remains polynomial-time computable. $\square$

This is why a chain of mathematical results can establish a large number of hardness statements without proving each pair from scratch. The *direction* of every link must nevertheless be correct.

## Section 5 — Hardness versus membership versus completeness

Let $\mathcal C$ be a collection of decision languages. A language B is **$\mathcal C$-hard** under polynomial-time many-one reductions when every $A\in\mathcal C$ satisfies $A\le_m^P B$. It is **$\mathcal C$-complete** if it is both $\mathcal C$-hard and a member of $\mathcal C$.

In particular, NP-hardness is a universal **incoming-reduction** condition, while NP membership asks for bounded polynomial-time verifiers (C04). Neither condition alone establishes NP-completeness.

SAT is a canonical NP-complete decision problem. That statement relies on the Cook–Levin theorem, whose proof supplies reductions from arbitrary nondeterministic polynomial-time computations to Boolean satisfiability. Our purpose here is to understand what that theorem asserts, not to treat its proof as already supplied.

## Section 6 — Many-one is not the only reduction

A polynomial-time **Turing reduction** may query a B oracle many times, choosing later queries based on earlier answers. For example, testing whether a finite undirected graph is connected can ask a reachability oracle whether a chosen root reaches each vertex. This procedure uses multiple queries and is an easy illustration of a Turing reduction; it does **not**, by itself, prove a single-output many-one reduction.

Different reduction types transport different conclusions. Outside complexity theory, computable reductions will reappear in C20–C21, with *computability* rather than polynomial running time as the translation constraint. A mathematically rigorous atlas must state the reduction notion, especially when comparing very different computational resources.

## Section 7 — A practical warning

Polynomial-time is an asymptotic category, not a guarantee of a good implementation. A reduction that expands a thousand-bit instance into $10^{12}$ bits may be polynomial but operationally disastrous. Lower-order factors and constants matter in engineering. Theoretically valid reductions remain valuable because their conclusion concerns scaling and logical implications, not deployment convenience.

## Exercises

1. Write the undirected-to-directed reachability map explicitly for a triangle and a disconnected graph; prove the two directions of correctness.
2. Suppose $A\le_m^P B$ in $O(n^3)$ time and B has an $O(m^4)$-time algorithm. Derive a polynomial worst-case upper bound for deciding A, and explain what output-length assumption you used.
3. Explain why a proposed reduction preserving yes-instances but not no-instances is invalid.
4. If $A\le_m^P B$ and $B\le_m^P C$, produce the composed translator and proof of correctness.
5. Diagnose the mistaken inference “A reduces to B, therefore A is harder than B.”
6. Distinguish the claims “B is in NP”, “B is NP-hard”, and “B is NP-complete”; which combinations imply the others?
