## Question: can we prove that some computable tasks truly require more time?

For SAT, we know an algorithm that tries all assignments, but we do not know whether every algorithm needs more than polynomial time. That uncertainty can create the misleading impression that complexity theory cannot prove *any* nontrivial limits on speed.

It can. When we compare larger, carefully defined time and space budgets, **hierarchy theorems** establish strict class separations. The difference is between proving *there exists some problem requiring more resources* and proving that a *particular familiar problem* does.

The larger classes also force us to separate the duration of a computation from the memory it needs. An exponentially long computation may fit in polynomial space, while a machine with exponentially many working cells may explore a vastly larger collection of configurations.

## Section 1 — Exponential time is measured against encoded input length

Let $n=|x|$ denote the bit length of the input. For any nonnegative function $t$, $\mathrm{DTIME}(t(n))$ is the class of decision languages accepted by a deterministic machine that halts on all inputs of length $n$ within $O(t(n))$ steps, with the usual fixed machine-model conventions.

Define

$$
\mathrm{EXPTIME}=\bigcup_{k\ge1}\mathrm{DTIME}(2^{n^k}).
$$

An EXPTIME algorithm may take exponentially many steps in some fixed polynomial of $n$; the exponent $k$ is a **constant independent of the input**, just as in the definition of P.

Likewise,

$$
\mathrm{EXPSPACE}=\bigcup_{k\ge1}\mathrm{DSPACE}(2^{n^k}).
$$

And a doubly exponential time class is

$$
\mathrm{2EXPTIME}=\bigcup_{k\ge1}\mathrm{DTIME}(2^{2^{n^k}}).
$$

Many equivalent formulations use $2^{n^{O(1)}}$ or $2^{2^{n^{O(1)}}}$. Constant factors and lower-order adjustments in the exponent can be absorbed by a larger fixed polynomial bound. The formal classes do not charge unit cost for arithmetic on arbitrarily long integers.

**Encoding example.** The integer $N=2^k$ has a binary encoding of length $k+1$. Counting through all $N$ values takes $2^k$ increments, an exponential number relative to that bit length, even though the counter needs only $O(k)$ bits. As in C10, this describes a *particular procedure*, not an unconditional lower bound on the problem of computing its final value.

## Section 2 — From a time bound to a space bound

A sequential machine can touch at most a bounded number of previously unused work cells in one step. Thus a deterministic computation taking at most $t(n)$ steps also uses at most $O(t(n))$ space. In particular,

$$
P\subseteq PSPACE,\qquad
\mathrm{EXPTIME}\subseteq\mathrm{EXPSPACE}.
$$

The converse does not follow by measuring a single execution step. Reusing a modest amount of space can permit very long computations.

There is, however, a useful deterministic upper bound on the running time of a **halting** space-bounded decider. A machine using $s(n)$ work cells has at most

$$
2^{O(s(n)+\log n)}
$$

configurations on an input of length $n$, under a fixed finite-alphabet, fixed-number-of-tapes machine model and for $s(n)\ge\log n$. A **complete configuration** specifies the machine's finite-control state, the input-head position, the contents of *all* currently occupied work cells and every work-tape head position. There are $O(1)$ possible control states, at most $n+O(1)$ possible input-head positions (hence $O(\log n)$ bits), at most $2^{O(s(n))}$ choices of work-tape contents, and at most $(s(n)+O(1))^{O(1)}$ choices of work-head positions (hence $O(\log(s(n)+1))$ additional bits). With $s(n)\ge\log n$, this gives the displayed $2^{O(s(n)+\log n)}$ bound.

A repeat of **just the work-tape snapshot** would not suffice: distinct head positions or control states may lead to different next steps. But if a deterministic machine repeats the *identical complete configuration* on the same fixed input, all subsequent behavior repeats identically, forcing an infinite loop. Since the decider halts on every input, its run cannot revisit such a configuration, and its duration is bounded by their finite number.

This argument yields

$$
PSPACE\subseteq EXPTIME
$$

and, for exponentially bounded space,

$$
EXPSPACE\subseteq 2EXPTIME.
$$

The configuration-count proof shows why small-space algorithms may take vastly more time. It is not a claim that an exponential-space machine necessarily consumes doubly exponential time on each input.

## Section 3 — The containment map

Combining the bounds gives a proved chain:

$$
P\subseteq PSPACE\subseteq EXPTIME
\subseteq EXPSPACE\subseteq 2EXPTIME.
$$

NP also lies between P and PSPACE, as C04 and C10 showed.

**Do not draw every arrow as strict.** The known endpoint separations are stronger than what is known about all adjacent pairs. In particular, whether $PSPACE=EXPTIME$ is open; a strict gap between these two is not a theorem.

The hierarchy theorems prove that, for sufficiently separated and constructible budgets, more resources genuinely allow additional decision languages. Their exact hypotheses matter.

## Section 4 — A proved time separation

**Theorem (deterministic time hierarchy, specialized consequence).**

$$
P\subsetneq EXPTIME.
$$

**Why this follows.** Deterministic time hierarchy establishes that for appropriate time-constructible bounds $t$ and $T$ with the smaller bound sufficiently below $T$, there are languages decidable within $T$ time but not within $t$ time.

A diagonal construction can simulate the $i$th candidate machine on an appropriately chosen encoded input and deliberately disagree with its output, while allowing enough overhead to perform the simulation. Choosing an exponential time budget that eventually dominates every fixed polynomial lets one separate a language in EXPTIME from the union of all polynomial-time machines.

The full hierarchy theorem includes time-constructibility and simulation-overhead conditions. This chapter uses the classical result rather than presenting the informal diagonal sketch as a complete theorem proof.

**Source.** Juris Hartmanis and Richard E. Stearns, *On the Computational Complexity of Algorithms*, *Transactions of the American Mathematical Society* 117 (1965), 285–306, [DOI:10.1090/S0002-9947-1965-0170805-7](https://doi.org/10.1090/S0002-9947-1965-0170805-7).

The separation proves that **some** computational problem needs superpolynomial deterministic time. It does **not** prove $P\ne NP$: the separation could occur after NP in the longer chain.

## Section 5 — A proved space separation

**Theorem (deterministic space hierarchy; imported).** For space-constructible functions $f(n),g(n)$ with $f(n),g(n)\ge\log n$ and $f(n)=o(g(n))$, the standard deterministic tape model satisfies the strict inclusion

$$
\mathrm{DSPACE}(f(n))\subsetneq\mathrm{DSPACE}(g(n)).
$$

*Space-constructible* means that a machine can establish its $f(n)$-cell workspace bound on input length $n$ while using at most $O(f(n))$ working cells, under the usual machine-model convention. The hypothesis $f=o(g)$ is a genuine asymptotic **gap**, not merely $f(n)\le g(n)$. For example, $f(n)=2^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}$ and $g(n)=2^n$ are space-constructible and satisfy $f=o(g)$. The hierarchy theorem yields a language inside $\mathrm{DSPACE}(2^n)$ but outside $\mathrm{DSPACE}(2^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor})$. Every fixed polynomial $n^k$ is eventually at most $2^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}$, so that language is outside **all** polynomial-space classes. Thus

$$
PSPACE\subsetneq EXPSPACE.
$$

This is an imported diagonalization theorem, not something proved by the finite counter example. **Primary source:** Richard E. Stearns, Juris Hartmanis and Philip M. Lewis II, [*Hierarchies of Memory Limited Computations*](https://doi.org/10.1109/FOCS.1965.11), *Sixth Annual Symposium on Switching Circuit Theory and Logical Design* (1965), 179–190. **Standard reference:** Sanjeev Arora and Boaz Barak, [*Computational Complexity: A Modern Approach*, Chapter 4, Space Complexity](https://doi.org/10.1017/CBO9780511804090.007), especially the space hierarchy theorem and constructibility conditions.

The strict result states that an exponential workspace permits decision of languages not decidable within any fixed polynomial workspace. It does not identify a particular familiar problem with this lower bound.

This also does not identify which intermediate resource classes differ. In particular, because

$$
PSPACE\subseteq EXPTIME\subseteq EXPSPACE,
$$

the strict difference between PSPACE and EXPSPACE establishes that **at least one** of those two steps is strict; it does not establish $PSPACE\ne EXPTIME$.

Likewise, deterministic time hierarchy gives

$$
EXPTIME\subsetneq 2EXPTIME.
$$

These are statements about entire collections of languages, with no need to attach every hard language to one named combinatorial puzzle.

## Section 6 — Computation with an alternating opponent

C11 introduced alternating choice as a game. If the two players alternate Boolean choices along a computation, then a polynomial-length play can branch into exponentially many possible plays. The classical alternation theorem says

$$
AP=PSPACE,
$$

where AP means alternating **polynomial time**.

What if an alternating Turing machine may instead use **polynomial space along each individual computation branch**? In the classical, finite-branching, existential/universal-state model, with the input read-only and all branches required to halt according to the decider's acceptance semantics, the imported alternation theorem says

$$
APSPACE=EXPTIME.
$$

Here $APSPACE$ is the union of alternating $O(n^k)$-*space* decision classes over fixed $k$, not a demand that an entire exponentially branching computation tree fit into polynomial total memory. A branch's workspace bound constrains the information stored at **one node**, not the **number of nodes** in the tree, which may be exponentially or doubly exponentially large. This distinction matters even when every individual branch uses only polynomial work cells.

**Caution.** A polynomial-time reduction cannot simply write out the whole computation tree. $APSPACE=EXPTIME$ is a theorem about effective alternating-machine simulations under stated resource conventions, not an assertion that the game tree has polynomial size. The chapter imports the theorem from Chandra–Kozen–Stockmeyer and does not supply a full simulation proof.

**Primary source:** Ashok K. Chandra, Dexter C. Kozen and Larry J. Stockmeyer, *Alternation*, *Journal of the ACM* 28 (1981), 114–133, [DOI:10.1145/322234.322243](https://doi.org/10.1145/322234.322243).

## Section 7 — A concrete resource comparison

Consider a machine executing the following conceptual loop on input $1$ followed by $k$ zero bits, encoding $2^k$:

1. Allocate a $k$-bit counter initialized to zero.
2. Repeat exactly $2^k$ times: increment the counter modulo $2^k$.
3. Output the final counter value.

The execution makes an exponential number of increments relative to input length $k+1$, yet keeps a counter of only $k$ bits and constant additional control information. An ordinary bit-cost implementation of each increment may take up to $O(k)$ bit operations, so the total time is at most $O(k2^k)$ and is at least $2^k$ operations.

This is an explicit example of **time-space asymmetry**. It is not evidence that the output cannot be calculated in polynomial time by some *different* procedure: in fact, the final value is always zero. The lower bound applies to this specified loop, not to its mathematical output task.

The difference between a bound for an **algorithm** and a bound for an **entire decision language** must remain visible throughout the Atlas.

## Section 8 — Why bigger class names do not solve P versus NP

We know

$$
P\subseteq NP\subseteq PSPACE\subseteq EXPTIME
$$

and the strict inequality $P\ne EXPTIME$. Thus the chain cannot consist entirely of equalities.

But there are logically different possibilities compatible with these containments. For example, the classes P and NP could coincide while PSPACE or EXPTIME remains larger. Another possibility is that P differs from NP. The hierarchy theorem alone does not distinguish them.

The existence of EXPTIME-complete decision problems also does not automatically show that SAT is EXPTIME-hard. Complexity class **membership**, **hardness**, and **provable separation** are different claims, requiring different kinds of proof.

When researching any named game or logic problem, the encoding matters. Generalized board games, succinct inputs and parameter-sized instances may receive very different classifications depending on what is counted as the input and whether a move/position is given explicitly. The Atlas will not import a completeness headline without the exact problem contract.

## Section 9 — Common mistakes

- **“Exponential in a number means exponential in its bit length.”** Not always; the numerical parameter and encoding length must be separated.
- **“An EXPTIME-complete problem cannot be in PSPACE.”** That would imply a presently unproved separation between PSPACE and EXPTIME.
- **“A computation using polynomial space is fast.”** Space can be reused across exponentially many steps.
- **“A hierarchy theorem tells us SAT needs exponential time.”** It establishes existence of languages outside smaller classes, not such a lower bound for SAT.
- **“Every inclusion in the containment chain is strict.”** Only specific separations have been proved.
- **“An entire computation tree can be encoded by writing down every node.”** Such an encoding may be superpolynomial; reductions require polynomial-size outputs.

## Map update

The Atlas now has a **proved separation layer** beyond the familiar open P-versus-NP question. Deterministic time hierarchy yields $P\subsetneq EXPTIME$ and $EXPTIME\subsetneq 2EXPTIME$; deterministic space hierarchy yields $PSPACE\subsetneq EXPSPACE$. The exact position of some intermediate boundaries—including PSPACE versus EXPTIME—remains open.

Space reuse explains how exponentially long executions can occupy modest memory. Alternation explains a second route from space-limited games to time-limited algorithms. Neither observation licenses drawing unproved strict containments.
