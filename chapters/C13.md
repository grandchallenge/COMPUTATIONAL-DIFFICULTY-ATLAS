## Question: is it harder to count answers than to find out whether any exist?

A satisfying assignment is evidence that a Boolean formula is satisfiable. If all we want to know is whether **at least one** assignment exists, one witness is enough. But suppose the task is to determine **exactly how many** assignments satisfy the formula.

For the formula

$$
\varphi=(a\lor b)
$$

over the explicitly specified variables $\{a,b\}$, three assignments satisfy it: $(0,1)$, $(1,0)$ and $(1,1)$. The decision answer is one bit, YES; the counting answer is the integer $3$.

This chapter introduces a complexity class of functions, **#P**, and a related class of decision languages, **PP**. It also shows a striking phenomenon: deciding whether a bipartite graph has a perfect matching is polynomial-time solvable, while counting every perfect matching is #P-complete.

The two formulations require different output contracts and cannot be conflated.

## 1. Count carefully: which variables are being assigned?

For a Boolean formula $\varphi$ with a declared finite variable set $V$, let

$$
\#SAT(\varphi,V)=
\left|\left\{\alpha:V\to\{0,1\}:\varphi(\alpha)=1\right\}\right|.
$$

Every variable's possible value counts, including variables in $V$ that do not appear in the formula. If the variable set is defined to be exactly the set of variables appearing in $\varphi$, that becomes part of the input convention.

**Worked example.** Over $\{a,b\}$, $\varphi=a\lor b$ has count $3$, but over $\{a,b,c\}$ it has count $6$: the unused variable $c$ has two choices for each satisfying pair. A reduction that adds unused variables can therefore preserve satisfiability **without** preserving the exact number of solutions.

For an unsatisfiable formula the count is zero. The decision predicate is simply

$$
SAT(\varphi,V)=\mathrm{YES}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\#SAT(\varphi,V)>0.
$$

**Important:** outputting the count in binary may require up to $|V|+1$ bits because it is at most $2^{|V|}$. The large *value* of the answer does not by itself force an exponentially long output. Any lower-bound argument must concern the computation, not the number of printed digits alone.

## 2. Definition of #P

A nondeterministic polynomial-time machine branches over finitely many binary choices, then accepts or rejects. For a fixed input $x$, let $\#\mathrm{acc}_M(x)$ be the number of accepting computation **paths** of $M$.

**Definition.** A function $f:\{0,1\}^*\to\mathbb N$ belongs to #P if there is a nondeterministic polynomial-time machine $M$ for which

$$
f(x)=\#\mathrm{acc}_M(x)
$$

on every input $x$. A malformed encoding can yield zero accepting paths, provided that convention is part of the total function.

Equivalently, #P consists of functions counting polynomial-length witnesses satisfying a polynomial-time decidable predicate:

$$
f(x)=
\bigl|\{w\in\{0,1\}^{p(|x|)}:R(x,w)=1\}\bigr|
$$

for some fixed polynomial $p$ and appropriate efficient predicate $R$. Witness lengths of "at most" polynomial can be represented at one fixed padded length with unambiguous encoding, so we do not inadvertently count the same witness many times.

Here the **output is a natural number**, not a yes/no response. Therefore **#P is not a subclass of NP** in the ordinary sense: NP is a class of decision languages, whereas #P is a class of numerical functions. Statements comparing them must first specify a conversion, such as a positive-count decision language or an oracle.

For a formula with $m$ variables, guessing one assignment and accepting precisely when it satisfies the formula yields one accepting path per satisfying assignment. Thus #SAT is in #P.

## 3. Why reduction types matter more for counting

C05 introduced polynomial-time many-one reductions preserving yes/no answers. Those are appropriate for NP-completeness but do **not** automatically preserve how many witnesses exist.

A **parsimonious reduction** from counting problem $f$ to counting problem $g$ is a polynomial-time input transformation $h$ satisfying

$$
f(x)=g(h(x))
$$

for every input $x$. No factor is lost, no witnesses are duplicated, and none are silently discarded.

Other counting reductions permit polynomial-time postprocessing or oracle calls; different #P-completeness statements may use different reduction notions. A published completeness result must say which is used. In this chapter, "parsimonious" will always mean literal preservation of the entire count.

**Toy warning.** Adding an unconstrained Boolean variable to a formula preserves whether it is satisfiable, but doubles the number of satisfying assignments. This is a valid decision reduction and **not** parsimonious for counting.

The standard tableau/circuit encodings can be designed so that auxiliary variables are uniquely determined by a choice of witness. Such a *uniqueness of extension* proof is the critical counting requirement missing from a generic SAT-equivalence argument.

**Classical theorem (imported):** #SAT is #P-complete under standard parsimonious polynomial-time reductions, with suitable explicit witness and encoding conventions. The chapter does not pretend that the preceding toy example proves the complete construction.

## 4. A surprising example: perfect matchings

A *perfect matching* in a bipartite graph is a collection of disjoint edges covering every vertex exactly once. Given a bipartite graph with the two sides of equal size, determining **whether one exists** can be done in polynomial time with matching algorithms.

But **counting** all perfect matchings has a more difficult classification.

Write the graph as a $0$–$1$ adjacency matrix $A=(a_{ij})$ with $m$ vertices on each side. Each perfect matching corresponds to a permutation $\sigma$ assigning one right-side vertex to every left-side vertex while respecting the edges.

The number of perfect matchings is the **permanent**:

$$
\operatorname{perm}(A)=
\sum_{\sigma\in S_m}
\prod_{i=1}^m a_{i,\sigma(i)}.
$$

Unlike the determinant, the permanent has **no alternating permutation signs**. Thus ordinary Gaussian elimination for determinants is not a direct polynomial-time algorithm for computing the permanent.

**Worked matrix.**

$$
A=
\begin{pmatrix}
1&1&0\\
1&1&1\\
0&1&1
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

The permitted perfect matchings, in row-to-column permutation notation, are $(1,2,3)$, $(1,3,2)$, and $(2,1,3)$, for a permanent of $3$. The determinant of this particular matrix is $-1$, illustrating why the two numerical tasks cannot simply be substituted for one another.

**Classical theorem (Valiant, imported).** Computing the permanent of a $0$–$1$ matrix is #P-complete (under the appropriate counting reductions). It follows that merely finding a perfect matching and counting all perfect matchings have sharply different known completeness classifications, even though neither observation by itself proves an unconditional separation between their polynomial-time solvabilities.

**Source:** Leslie G. Valiant, *The Complexity of Computing the Permanent*, *Theoretical Computer Science* 8 (1979), 189–201, [DOI:10.1016/0304-3975(79)90044-6](https://doi.org/10.1016/0304-3975(79)90044-6). The reductions in that paper are not reproduced in full by our three-by-three example.

## 5. Why PP is a decision class

A different question asks whether a majority of randomly chosen computation paths accept.

**Definition.** A language belongs to PP if a polynomial-time probabilistic Turing machine with a fixed polynomial number of uniformly random bits accepts each yes-input with probability **strictly greater than** $1/2$, and accepts each no-input with probability **at most** $1/2$.

Equivalently, some nondeterministic polynomial-time machine's accepting paths form a strict majority of its equally many total computation paths. The strict inequality is essential: an exact tie is a NO outcome under this convention.

This is a class of **decision languages**, unlike #P. PP's majority threshold is **not** the bounded-error gap defining BPP. A PP yes-instance could have acceptance probability $1/2+2^{-p(n)}$, and a no-instance might be exactly $1/2$. Repeated sampling need not distinguish those cases in polynomial time.

**Tiny example.** A machine using three random bits has eight equally likely paths. Four accepting paths do not make a PP yes-instance; five do.

## 6. NP lies in PP: a constructive proof

**Theorem.** $NP\subseteq PP$.

**Proof.** Let $L$ be decided by a nondeterministic polynomial-time machine $M$ whose computation branches can be padded to exactly $2^{p(n)}$ equally weighted paths for some polynomial $p$. The input belongs to $L$ precisely when at least one of these paths accepts.

Construct a probabilistic polynomial-time machine $N$. It samples one extra fair bit $b$, then samples $p(n)$ additional fair bits.

- If $b=0$, it accepts regardless of the remaining bits.
- If $b=1$, it uses the remaining bits to specify one of $M$'s paths and accepts exactly when $M$ does.

There are $2^{p(n)+1}$ equally weighted random choices. If $a(x)$ of $M$'s original paths accept, then exactly

$$
2^{p(n)}+a(x)
$$

paths of $N$ accept. Therefore $N$ accepts with probability greater than $1/2$ exactly when $a(x)>0$, that is, exactly when $x\in L$. All simulations and random-bit generation take polynomial time. $\square$

The construction is a rigorous inclusion proof. It does **not** show that SAT belongs to BPP or that PP can be solved deterministically in polynomial time.

## 7. Counting and threshold questions are different

For #SAT, we want an exact integer; for SAT, we ask whether that integer is positive; for a majority version, we compare it with an explicitly specified threshold, often half the assignments.

Given a formula on $m$ variables, the majority predicate

$$
\#SAT(\varphi)>2^{m-1}
$$

is a representative PP-type decision problem under the appropriate exact encoding and reduction conventions.

Although counting is conceptually richer than existence, one must not assert an unconditional separation between the **function** class FP (polynomial-time computable numerical functions) and #P without acknowledging its connection to unresolved complexity questions. Proving #SAT outside FP would settle a difficult class separation.

Likewise, PP and PSPACE are both decision classes. Known upper bounds include

$$
NP\subseteq PP\subseteq PSPACE.
$$

The second inclusion follows because polynomially many random bits give at most exponentially many paths; one can enumerate and count their acceptances using polynomial working space and a polynomial-length binary counter, even though it may take exponential time. These containments are not asserted strict.

## 8. What counting complexity reveals—and what it does not

Counting tasks can be central in reliability, combinatorics, probabilistic inference and statistical mechanics. In those applications, the exact number of configurations matters even when mere existence is easy to determine.

However, a problem being #P-complete under a standard reduction does not imply every instance is hard, or that no useful approximations or randomized estimators exist. Those are separate algorithmic promises with their own error and distributional requirements.

The larger Atlas will distinguish exact counting from approximate counting, worst-case hardness from average-case inference, and decision thresholds from numerical output. The mere presence of a probability in a problem statement does not automatically place it in BPP.

## 9. Common errors

- **“#P is NP with a hashtag.”** #P consists of numerical functions; NP consists of languages.
- **“One solution implies the answer is 1.”** Existence gives no information about how many other solutions exist.
- **“A SAT-preserving reduction preserves #SAT.”** Not without a parsimonious or suitably adjusted counting construction.
- **“Counting the permanent is as easy as taking a determinant.”** The signs make them different functions.
- **“PP means polynomial probability or bounded-error computation.”** PP uses a strict *majority* threshold, possibly with an exponentially tiny gap.
- **“The complete counting problem is unconditionally outside FP.”** No such separation is established by a completeness theorem alone.
- **“NP ⊆ PP ⊆ PSPACE must be strict.”** The containments are known; those particular strictness claims are not.
