## Question: what if it is easy to disprove a claim?

A Boolean formula is a tautology when it is true under **every** assignment. To refute a claim of tautology, one need only exhibit **one** assignment making the formula false. The witness may be easy to check even if no efficient algorithm is known for deciding tautology in general.

This is the central insight of the class **coNP**. NP formalizes decision problems with short, efficiently checkable *yes*-witnesses. coNP formalizes decision problems whose *no*-instances admit such witnesses, because the **complement** language is in NP.

The word "co" means complement, not cooperation, co-computation, or a different physical computational resource.

## Section 1 — Complement means reversing membership on all strings

A decision language is a set $L\subseteq\{0,1\}^*$. Its complement is

$$
\overline L=\{0,1\}^*\setminus L.
$$

Thus for **every binary string**, including malformed encodings, exactly one of $L$ or $\overline L$ contains it.

**Definition.** The class coNP consists of all decision languages $L$ whose complements belong to NP:

$$
coNP=\{L\subseteq\{0,1\}^*:\overline L\in NP\}.
$$

Equivalently, for $L\in coNP$ there are a polynomial $p$ and a deterministic polynomial-time verifier $V$ such that

$$
x\notin L
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\exists w,\ |w|\le p(|x|)\ \text{and}\ V(x,w)=1.
$$

A coNP verifier in this formulation is a witness checker for **nonmembership**. It is not a polynomial-time algorithm that necessarily decides the original language.

There is a small encoding subtlety. If SAT is defined to reject malformed strings, then its exact complement contains both valid unsatisfiable formulas and malformed strings. Some textbooks call the *valid-unsatisfiable-formula language* UNSAT; it excludes malformed inputs. Those variants differ only by a polynomial-time detectable validity case, and both belong to coNP and are coNP-complete under the standard conventions. One must nevertheless avoid writing that they are literally the same set of binary strings without specifying the malformed-input convention.

## Section 2 — Tautology, satisfiability, and one falsifying assignment

For an ordinary Boolean formula $\varphi$:

- $\varphi$ is **satisfiable** if at least one assignment makes it true;
- $\varphi$ is **unsatisfiable** if no assignment makes it true;
- $\varphi$ is a **tautology** if every assignment makes it true.

The logical identities are

$$
\varphi\text{ unsatisfiable}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\neg\varphi\text{ tautological}
$$

and

$$
\varphi\text{ not tautological}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\neg\varphi\text{ satisfiable}.
$$

A falsifying assignment is a short witness that a valid formula is **not** a tautology. Each assignment has at most one truth bit per variable, and evaluating it requires polynomial time in the formula's encoded length. Consequently TAUT is in coNP.

As a small example,

$$
\psi=(a\lor\neg a)\land(b\lor\neg b)
$$

is a tautology. In contrast,

$$
\theta=(a\lor b)\land(\neg a\lor b)
$$

is satisfiable (take $b=1$), but **not** a tautology (take $a=0,b=0$). That single falsifying assignment efficiently refutes the universal claim.

Finally, $\chi=a\land\neg a$ is unsatisfiable. Its negation, $\neg\chi$, is a tautology. These examples distinguish the three logical properties without relying on complexity assumptions.

## Section 3 — Why SAT and TAUT have different certificates

To demonstrate that a formula is satisfiable, supply one assignment that makes it true. This is a yes-witness for SAT.

To demonstrate that a formula is *not* a tautology, supply one assignment that makes it false. This is a yes-witness for the complement of TAUT.

To demonstrate that a formula **is** a tautology, checking one or a few assignments does not suffice: the claim is universal. Likewise, showing that a formula is unsatisfiable requires ruling out every possible satisfying assignment, even though some instances admit very short formal refutations.

Proof complexity makes this distinction precise. A **Cook–Reckhow proof system** has a polynomial-time verification relation whose range is exactly the set of propositional tautologies. The existence of *some polynomially bounded* such system (every tautology has a proof of length polynomial in its own length) is equivalent to $NP=coNP$; this equivalence does **not** establish that either class equality holds. Particular restricted proof systems can have proven superpolynomial lower bounds. The definition of coNP assumes only short falsifying assignments for **non-tautologies**, not polynomially sized proofs for every tautology. Cook and Reckhow, *The Relative Efficiency of Propositional Proof Systems* (1979), [DOI:10.2307/2273702](https://doi.org/10.2307/2273702), is the foundational reference.

This is a distinction between the *logical direction* of a witness and a universal algorithmic lower bound. We have not proved that tautology checking requires exponential time.

## Section 4 — Basic containment facts

**Theorem.** $P\subseteq NP\cap coNP$.

**Proof.** If $L\in P$, then C04's empty-witness verifier construction shows $L\in NP$. C03 proved that P is closed under complement, so $\overline L\in P\subseteq NP$. By the definition of coNP, $L\in coNP$ as well. Hence $L$ lies in both classes. $\square$

Also, complementation exchanges NP and coNP by definition:

$$
L\in NP\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\overline L\in coNP.
$$

**Unknown boundaries.** It is not known whether $P=NP$; it is also not known whether $NP=coNP$. In particular, the diagrams must **not** show NP and coNP as disjoint, or place either strictly above the other.

If $P=NP$, then coNP also equals P, because P is closed under complement. That is an implication from a hypothetical equality, not a proved strict separation.

## Section 5 — Completeness transfers to complements

The correct counterpart of NP-hardness uses the same polynomial-time many-one reductions.

**Definition.** A language $H$ is coNP-hard if every $L\in coNP$ satisfies $L\le_m^P H$. It is coNP-complete if it is both coNP-hard and in coNP.

**Theorem.** Suppose $A$ is NP-complete. Then its exact complement $\overline A$ is coNP-complete.

**Proof.** As $A\in NP$, the definition immediately gives $\overline A\in coNP$.

Now fix any $L\in coNP$. Its complement $\overline L$ belongs to NP. Because $A$ is NP-hard, choose a polynomial-time reduction $f$ satisfying

$$
x\in\overline L
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
f(x)\in A.
$$

Negate both membership statements:

$$
x\in L
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
f(x)\in\overline A.
$$

The **same** polynomial-time translator $f$ is therefore a reduction from $L$ to $\overline A$. This holds for every coNP language, proving coNP-hardness. $\square$

By Cook–Levin (cited in C06), SAT is NP-complete, so its exact complement is coNP-complete. The conventional language of valid unsatisfiable formulas is also coNP-complete once the encoding-validity convention is handled explicitly.

## Section 6 — TAUT is coNP-complete

For a valid Boolean formula $\varphi$, construct the formula $\neg\varphi$ by adding one negation node. This increases the description length by only a constant number of symbols under an ordinary parse-tree encoding.

Then

$$
\varphi\text{ unsatisfiable}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\neg\varphi\text{ tautological}.
$$

This is a polynomial-time answer-preserving reduction from UNSAT to TAUT on valid formulas. If the source language uses the **exact complement** of SAT, first test whether its input is a valid formula. For an invalid input, output a fixed tautology, because invalid strings are yes-instances of that exact complement. For a valid input, output $\neg\varphi$.

Together with the coNP-completeness of the SAT complement, this makes TAUT coNP-hard. We already showed TAUT is in coNP, so it is **coNP-complete**.

The proof does not infer coNP-completeness merely from "tautologies seem difficult." It provides a source complete problem, a polynomial translator, both membership implications, and the certificate characterization.

## Section 7 — A tempting but invalid inference

A common argument says: "SAT is NP-complete and its complement is coNP-complete, therefore NP and coNP are different." The conclusion does not follow. Naming two descriptions of a complementary pair of languages does not prove that either entire class is distinct.

Another tempting inference says that **if** NP equals coNP, then a polynomial-time algorithm for SAT must exist. The equality alone would provide symmetric forms of short verifiable certificates, but no known argument turns those certificates into a deterministic polynomial-time solver. The implication to $P=NP$ is not established by the basic definitions.

The state of knowledge is more limited: we know $P\subseteq NP\cap coNP$, know that SAT and TAUT are complete for their respective classes, and do **not** know the class equalities.

## Section 8 — Beyond formula examples

Some problems are known to lie in $NP\cap coNP$ without being known to lie in P. Such intersections can be important evidence about certificate structures, but the precise status of familiar examples can change as better algorithms are discovered.

**Integer factoring illustrates the formulation caution.** Finding the prime factors of an integer is a search/function task. It is not literally a member of NP, coNP, or P without an associated decision formulation. Certain decision questions connected to factoring have certificates for yes and no answers; their precise class status must be stated for the actual encoded language, not inferred from the word "factoring."

We therefore keep the intersection story distinct from the hardness story, and we resist treating classes as a universal ranking of practical difficulty.

4. Starting from $L\in P$, prove $L\in NP\cap coNP$.
5. Let $A$ be NP-complete. Reproduce the argument that $\overline A$ is coNP-complete, carefully retaining the direction of every reduction arrow.
