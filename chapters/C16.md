## Question: can more processors always make a problem fast?

Suppose eight people are asked to calculate the parity of eight bits—the exclusive OR of all bits. One person can combine the bits in sequence. But four people can combine pairs at the same time, then two can combine the four intermediate answers, and finally one person combines the last two answers.

Both approaches perform seven XOR operations. They differ in the length of the longest chain of dependencies: seven sequential steps versus three parallel rounds.

The distinction leads to **work** (total computation) and **depth** (the longest dependency chain). It also motivates NC, a class of problems admitting efficient highly parallel algorithms, and P-completeness, a notion of sequential difficulty defined by suitably weak reductions.

## 1. A computation is a directed acyclic dependency graph

Model a computation as a directed acyclic graph (DAG) of elementary operations. A node may execute only after its predecessor outputs are available. Different ready nodes can be processed concurrently.

- **Work $W$** is the total number of operations (unit-cost nodes under the chosen abstract model).
- **Depth $D$** is the maximum number of operations on any directed path through the dependency DAG.
- **Parallel time $T_p$** is the number of synchronous unit-time rounds using at most $p$ processors, under an appropriate scheduler.

Regardless of scheduling, we have the lower bounds

$$
T_p\ge \frac{W}{p}
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
T_p\ge D.
$$

The first says processors cannot collectively perform more than $p$ unit operations per round; the second says a dependency chain cannot be compressed by assigning its links to different processors.

A classical work–span scheduling theorem gives an upper bound of order

$$
T_p=O\!\left(\frac{W}{p}+D\right)
$$

for suitable greedy schedules and unit-time DAG nodes. Its constants and machine assumptions are important; real hardware adds communication, memory contention and synchronization. The equation describes an abstract parallel cost model, not a promised speedup on every device.

## 2. A complete worked example: parity of eight bits

Let the input be $b_1,\ldots,b_8\in\{0,1\}$ and define

$$
\mathrm{PARITY}(b_1,\ldots,b_8)=
b_1\oplus b_2\oplus\cdots\oplus b_8.
$$

A sequential XOR chain performs seven XOR gates in depth seven. A balanced tree computes:

$$
\begin{aligned}
u_1&=b_1\oplus b_2,\quad
u_2=b_3\oplus b_4,\quad
u_3=b_5\oplus b_6,\quad
u_4=b_7\oplus b_8,\\
v_1&=u_1\oplus u_2,\quad
v_2=u_3\oplus u_4,\\
z&=v_1\oplus v_2.
\end{aligned}
$$

Round 1 has four independent gates, round 2 has two, and round 3 has one. Hence $W=7$ and $D=3$ for this concrete tree. The output agrees with the XOR of all eight input bits because XOR is associative.

For $n=2^k$ input bits, the same balanced construction has

$$
W=n-1,\qquad D=\log_2 n.
$$

The input size includes $n$ bits, so a logarithmic depth in $n$ is much smaller than the original sequential chain's linear depth for large $n$.

This demonstrates **an efficient parallel construction**, not a universal lower bound on every implementation of parity. It also illustrates why adding more processors helps only when the dependency graph exposes parallel work.

## 3. Boolean circuit families and the class NC

For each input length $n$, consider a Boolean circuit $C_n$ of bounded fan-in (for example AND, OR, and NOT gates) computing a decision function on $n$ input bits. A *circuit family* contains one circuit for each length, rather than one infinite-size physical device.

A standard definition of NC is the union of classes $\mathrm{NC}^k$: languages decidable by **uniform** bounded-fan-in Boolean circuit families of polynomial size and depth $O((\log n)^k)$ for some fixed positive integer $k$.

The word **uniform** matters. If a different arbitrary circuit could be selected for each $n$ without any effective generation rule, the family might smuggle uncomputable information into the circuit descriptions. In this chapter, take a conventional DLOGTIME-uniform circuit-family definition; other common uniformity conventions require explicit comparison when citing theorems.

A circuit of polynomial size can be evaluated sequentially in polynomial time. Therefore

$$
NC\subseteq P.
$$

The reverse inclusion is an **open** class equality question. We do not know whether every deterministic polynomial-time language admits polynomial-size polylogarithmic-depth *uniform* circuits.

Because each XOR can be expressed by a constant-size bounded-fan-in AND/OR/NOT circuit, the parity tree illustrates membership in NC under a uniform construction. More sophisticated parallel algorithms may require polylogarithmic depth without a fixed simple tree.

## 4. Why logarithmic space and parallel time are different axes

A logspace machine uses only $O(\log n)$ work bits, but may run for polynomially many steps. A parallel circuit uses potentially polynomially many gates in total while restricting the longest dependency path.

Memory usage, total work, and parallel depth are different resource measures. It would be wrong to infer from a small-space algorithm that its straightforward execution automatically has polylogarithmic depth. Some logspace computations can be simulated efficiently in parallel by general theorems, but the simulation is itself a nontrivial mathematical result.

The Atlas should not collapse space, depth, and processor count into one picture labeled simply "difficulty."

## 5. Circuit Value: a canonical P-complete problem

The **Circuit Value Problem** (CVP) supplies an encoded acyclic Boolean circuit with its input bits specified, then asks whether the designated output gate evaluates to true.

There is an obvious polynomial-time sequential algorithm: parse the circuit, process gates in topological order, and compute each gate's value once. Thus CVP is in P.

A classical result says CVP is **P-complete under logarithmic-space many-one reductions**. In other words, every language in P can be transformed to a CVP instance by a particularly weak, logspace-bounded translator.

Why use logspace reductions instead of the ordinary polynomial-time reductions from C05? If arbitrary polynomial-time translators were allowed to perform the entire source computation before producing a fixed YES or NO circuit, the definition would not distinguish P-complete problems meaningfully. Using a weaker reduction notion preserves the distinction between efficient sequential computation and efficient parallel computation.

**Important conditional.** If CVP were in NC, then the logspace reductions and known parallel simulations of logspace would place all of P in NC. Coupled with $NC\subseteq P$, this would give $NC=P$. The reverse implication is immediate if $NC=P$, since CVP is in P.

This is an established conditional equivalence, **not** a proof that CVP lies outside NC or that NC differs from P.

**Historical source:** Richard E. Ladner, *The Circuit Value Problem Is Log Space Complete for P* (1975), *ACM SIGACT News* 7(1), 18–20. The original P-completeness theorem is imported here; the chapter does not claim the parity tree establishes it.

## 6. Why a deep supplied circuit is not a lower bound for its function

A straight chain of $n$ Boolean gates may have depth $n$ in its **given representation**. Yet the Boolean function it computes might also admit a completely different circuit of depth $O(\log n)$.

The parity example shows precisely this: a supplied sequential circuit for parity has linear depth, while a balanced equivalent circuit has logarithmic depth.

Therefore CVP's P-completeness statement concerns the problem of evaluating **arbitrary explicitly encoded circuits**, not an assertion that each deep circuit computes an intrinsically sequential function. Structural depth of one representation is not a universal lower bound on all equivalent algorithms.

This mirrors C08's broader warning: one slow algorithm never establishes a lower bound against all possible algorithms.

## 7. Work efficiency and actual machines

A parallel algorithm can have very small depth but enormous work. For instance, computing a huge number of possible candidates independently and then combining the answers may require exponentially many processors. That would not satisfy NC's **polynomial circuit-size** requirement, even if the combination depth were logarithmic in the number of candidates.

Good parallel algorithms control both:
- total work, to avoid replacing time cost with a colossal processor budget, and
- depth, to expose an efficient critical path.

The idealized work/depth model ignores interprocessor communication costs and synchronization latency. A lower-depth algorithm is not automatically faster on one real-world GPU or cluster. Complexity-theoretic "efficient parallelism" and engineering throughput should be evaluated separately.

## 8. What is proved, and what is unknown?

We have:
- a constructive logarithmic-depth parity circuit with linear work;
- the unconditional inclusion $NC\subseteq P$;
- the classical P-completeness of CVP under logspace reductions;
- the *conditional* consequence that a general NC algorithm for CVP would collapse NC and P.

We do **not** have a proof that $NC\ne P$. Nor does a parallel algorithm for one useful problem establish that every problem in P is efficiently parallelizable.

There are also choices among circuit uniformity, processor models, work/span conventions and reduction strength. A theorem without these choices may be misleading even when its rough intuition is useful.

## 9. Common mistakes

- **“More processors always yield linear speedup.”** Dependency depth places a lower bound independent of processor count.
- **“Logarithmic circuit depth is enough for NC.”** Circuit size must be polynomial and the family suitably uniform.
- **“A supplied deep circuit has an inherently deep function.”** A different equivalent circuit may be shallower.
- **“P-complete proves a problem cannot be parallelized.”** It establishes hardness relative to all of P; strict NC versus P remains open.
- **“Polynomial-time reductions are the correct test for P-completeness.”** They are too strong for the intended distinction; logspace (or another suitably weak) reductions are used.
- **“Low work implies low depth.”** A long chain has linear work and linear depth.
- **“Theoretical depth predicts GPU wall-clock speed.”** Communication and hardware architecture are not accounted for by the abstract model.

## Map update

The Atlas adds the **parallel-depth axis**. An efficient sequential algorithm may still have a long dependency chain; an efficient parallel algorithm requires small depth *and* manageable work/circuit size. NC captures polynomial-size polylogarithmic-depth uniform circuit families, known to sit inside P. P-complete problems such as Circuit Value express the unresolved frontier of efficient parallelization under weak reductions.

This is not a strict containment map: $NC\subseteq P$ is proved, while $NC\stackrel{?}{=}P$ remains open.
