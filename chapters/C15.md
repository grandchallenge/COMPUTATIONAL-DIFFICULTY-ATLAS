# C15 — Quantum Computation

**Status:** Manuscript candidate, not yet independently reviewed.

## Question: what is being measured?

Quantum algorithms use states, transformations, interference, and measurement, rather than simply enumerating and inspecting all possible answers. The class BQP contains decision languages solved by uniform polynomial-size quantum circuits with acceptance probability at least 2/3 on yes-instances and at most 1/3 on no-instances. Amplification reduces these error probabilities with polynomial overhead.

## Known boundaries

The proved inclusion is \(P\subseteq BQP\). Integer factorization and discrete logarithms have polynomial-time quantum algorithms using Shor-type methods; factoring is primarily a function/search problem and should not silently be treated as a decision-language classification.

Grover search requires \(O(\sqrt N)\) oracle queries for an unstructured database of N entries, compared with \(\Theta(N)\) bounded-error classical queries. This is a query-complexity result; physical runtime may include costly oracle implementation.

No general polynomial-time quantum algorithm for NP-complete SAT is known. In particular, neither \(NP\subseteq BQP\) nor a strict separation between them is established. Integer factorization is not known NP-complete, so Shor's algorithm does not solve the P versus NP question.

## Example and exercises

For a search domain of a million items, contrast one million classical oracle queries with approximately one thousand quantum oracle queries, suppressing constants. Explain why a quantum measurement does not reveal every amplitude. State why quantum factoring does not imply an efficient SAT algorithm. Distinguish an oracle-query improvement from a runtime improvement.
