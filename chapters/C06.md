## Question: can one problem represent the entire difficulty of NP?

A satisfiability instance asks whether some assignment makes a Boolean formula true. A graph clique instance asks whether some group of vertices are all connected to one another. The problems seem unrelated. Yet a carefully constructed translation can turn a satisfiability instance into a graph whose large cliques represent satisfying choices.

This is the point of **NP-completeness**: not a claim that one particular solver is slow, but a proof that solving the target problem efficiently would give efficient algorithms for **every** problem in NP.

The direction of the translation matters. The source problem is the one we already know to be hard; the target problem inherits that hardness.

## 1. Three distinct claims

Let $A$ and $B$ denote languages over encoded finite instances, and let $A\le_m^P B$ mean that a deterministic polynomial-time function $f$ satisfies

$$
x\in A\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad f(x)\in B
$$

for every encoded input $x$. C05 established why such a translator lets a solver for $B$ decide $A$.

**Definition: NP-hard.** A language $H$ is NP-hard under polynomial-time many-one reductions when $L\le_m^P H$ for **every** language $L\in NP$.

**Definition: NP-complete.** A language $C$ is NP-complete when (i) $C\in NP$ and (ii) $C$ is NP-hard.

These are separate proof obligations. For the first, supply a polynomially bounded witness and a deterministic polynomial-time verifier. For the second, use a known NP-hard source and a correct, polynomial-time, answer-preserving reduction to the target.

**Important:** NP-hard does not mean membership in NP. A problem may be NP-hard without possessing polynomial-length yes-certificates, and some NP-hard languages are undecidable. NP-complete is the intersection of the NP membership requirement and NP-hardness.

An arbitrary NP problem is not proved NP-complete merely because nobody has discovered an efficient algorithm.

## 2. Boolean satisfiability as the historical anchor

A Boolean formula is built from Boolean variables, negation, conjunction, and disjunction. It is **satisfiable** if at least one assignment of truth values makes the entire formula true.

For example,

$$
\varphi=(a\lor b)\land(\neg a\lor c)
$$

is satisfied by $a=\text{true}$ and $c=\text{true}$, regardless of $b$. A verifier can evaluate the given assignment by walking through the formula, so SAT lies in NP.

The **Cook–Levin theorem** proves the much stronger result that SAT is NP-hard, and hence NP-complete.

**Theorem (Cook–Levin, cited).** Every language in NP has a polynomial-time many-one reduction to SAT.

This is an established theorem being **used**, not a fully rederived theorem in this introductory chapter. The mechanism explains the relevance: a polynomial-time verifier has a polynomial-size accepting computation tableau or Boolean circuit description. Boolean variables represent the local computation's bits and constraints enforce legal transitions, consistency, and acceptance. There are only polynomially many local constraints, so the resulting formula is polynomial in the original input length and is satisfiable exactly when an accepting witness exists.

A rigorous full proof must account for the machine encoding, tableau size, constraints, and the polynomial-time reduction. Nothing in a handful of SAT examples supplies that proof. The later advanced notes may unpack the construction in detail.

Cook, *The Complexity of Theorem-Proving Procedures* (1971), DOI [10.1145/800157.805047](https://doi.org/10.1145/800157.805047); Leonid Levin's parallel foundational results on universal search and complexity (1973); Richard M. Karp, *Reducibility Among Combinatorial Problems* (1972), DOI [10.1007/978-1-4684-2001-2_9](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-1-4684-2001-2_9).

## 3. From SAT to 3SAT

A *literal* is a variable or its negation. A clause is a disjunction of literals, and a formula in conjunctive normal form (CNF) is a conjunction of clauses.

In **3SAT**, every clause has between one and three literals; a clause of one or two literals can be padded to exactly three by repeating literals. An empty clause makes the formula immediately unsatisfiable and is handled as a special case. An empty conjunction is satisfiable and likewise has a direct special case.

It is not enough simply to distribute conjunctions over disjunctions: unrestricted distribution can blow up a formula exponentially. Instead, introduce fresh variables for subformula gates.

For a conjunction gate $z\leftrightarrow(p\land q)$, the following CNF constraints enforce exactly its truth table:

$$
(\neg z\lor p)\land(\neg z\lor q)
 \land(z\lor\neg p\lor\neg q).
$$

For a disjunction gate $z\leftrightarrow(p\lor q)$, use

$$
(z\lor\neg p)\land(z\lor\neg q)
 \land(\neg z\lor p\lor q).
$$

For negation $z\leftrightarrow\neg p$, use

$$
(\neg z\lor\neg p)\land(z\lor p).
$$

Add a unit clause requiring the output variable to be true. Any satisfying assignment of the original circuit can be extended to the new gate variables, and any satisfying assignment of the constraints describes a consistent computation with true output. With a constant number of bounded-size clauses per gate, the transformation is polynomial.

Thus SAT reduces to 3SAT. Because SAT is NP-hard and 3SAT belongs to NP, 3SAT is NP-complete. The result follows from the cited Cook–Levin theorem and the explicit bounded-size gate construction, not from an unsupported assertion that CNF expansion is always cheap.

## 4. A complete reduction: 3SAT to CLIQUE

A *clique* of size $k$ in an undirected simple graph is a set of $k$ distinct vertices in which every two different vertices are adjacent.

Define

$$
\mathrm{CLIQUE}=
\{\langle G,k\rangle:G\text{ contains a clique of size at least }k\}.
$$

Membership in NP is immediate: a witness lists $k$ distinct vertex identifiers, and a verifier checks all $\binom{k}{2}$ pairs against the graph, with ordinary parsing and integer-size checks. If $k$ exceeds the vertex count, the instance is a no-instance.

Now let a 3CNF formula be

$$
\Phi=C_1\land C_2\land\cdots\land C_m.
$$

Construct a graph $G_\Phi$ with one vertex $(i,\ell)$ for each **literal occurrence** $\ell$ in clause $C_i$. Literal occurrences, not merely variable names, are separate vertices. Add an undirected edge between two vertices exactly when:

1. they belong to different clauses, and
2. their literals are not complementary (not a variable and its negation).

For the main construction assume $m\ge1$ and that every clause is nonempty. If any clause is empty, instead output a one-vertex graph with threshold $k=2$ (a fixed NO instance). If there are no clauses, output a one-vertex graph with threshold $k=1$ (a fixed YES instance). Otherwise the reduction outputs $\langle G_\Phi,m\rangle$. In particular, **never** connect two vertices from the same clause.

A three-literal clause gives at most three vertices, so there are at most $3m$ vertices and $O(m^2)$ potential edges. Constructing the occurrence list and deciding whether two literals contradict takes polynomial time in the encoded formula length. Unit and two-literal clauses work unchanged.

### Forward implication: satisfying assignment implies clique

Suppose $\Phi$ is satisfiable. In each clause choose a literal occurrence made true by the satisfying assignment. This gives exactly one selected vertex from every clause.

The selected literals cannot include both $x$ and $\neg x$, because a consistent assignment could not make both true. Therefore every pair of selected vertices comes from different clauses and is noncomplementary; the construction connects every pair. The $m$ selected vertices form a clique.

### Reverse implication: clique implies satisfying assignment

Suppose $G_\Phi$ has a clique of size $m$. No two vertices in the clique can come from the same clause, and there are only $m$ clauses. Hence the clique selects exactly one literal occurrence from **each** clause.

All selected literals are pairwise noncomplementary. They can therefore be made simultaneously true by assigning each mentioned variable the truth value demanded by its selected literal. Unmentioned variables may receive arbitrary truth values.

Each clause contains one selected true literal, so every clause is true and $\Phi$ is satisfiable.

We have proved

$$
\Phi\in\mathrm{3SAT}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\langle G_\Phi,m\rangle\in\mathrm{CLIQUE}.
$$

Together with the polynomial construction and 3SAT's NP-completeness, this shows CLIQUE is NP-hard; with the explicit witness verifier, CLIQUE is **NP-complete**.

## 5. Two tiny examples

**Satisfiable instance.** Let

$$
\Phi=(a\lor b\lor c)
\land(\neg a\lor b\lor c)
\land(a\lor\neg b\lor c).
$$

The assignment $c=\text{true}$ satisfies all three clauses. In the reduction graph, choose the three occurrences of literal $c$, one from each clause. They lie in different clauses and are pairwise noncontradictory, so they form a triangle, a clique of size three.

**Unsatisfiable instance.** Consider $\Psi=(a)\land(\neg a)$. Its graph has one occurrence vertex in each of two clauses. Since the two literals contradict, there is no edge between these vertices and hence no clique of size two. This illustrates the reduction's reverse direction in a minimal case.

The first example's triangle is an explicit witness. It does not imply that every 3CNF instance is satisfiable; the second example supplies the opposite outcome.

## 6. How hardness propagates

If $A$ is NP-hard and $A\le_m^P B$, then $B$ is NP-hard. Indeed, for any $L\in NP$, choose a polynomial-time reduction $L\le_m^P A$, compose it with the given reduction $A\le_m^P B$, and use C02's polynomial composition argument.

The **direction** is:

$$
\text{known NP-hard source}\ \longrightarrow\
\text{new target to prove hard}.
$$

The reverse implication is not generally valid. A polynomial-time reduction $A\le_m^P B$ establishes an *upper-bound transport* from a $B$ solver to an $A$ solver. It does not say that a fast solver for $A$ would efficiently solve $B$.

There are multiple reduction notions (many-one, Turing, randomized, approximation-preserving). A completeness claim is incomplete unless the reduction type is known; the NP-completeness statements here use deterministic polynomial-time **many-one** reductions.

## 7. What completeness would imply—and what it does not

If **any** NP-complete language belongs to P, then $P=NP$. The proof is short: every $L\in NP$ reduces to that complete language, and P is closed under efficient translations, so $NP\subseteq P$. The opposite containment $P\subseteq NP$ was already proved in C04.

Conversely, if $P=NP$, every NP-complete language is in P because it is a member of NP.

Neither direction decides whether $P=NP$ in actual mathematics. NP-complete problems have extensive practical algorithms and heuristics, but exponential worst-case behavior of familiar solvers is not an unconditional lower bound.

NP-complete also does not mean "most computationally difficult imaginable." Harder resource classes and undecidable problems occupy different mathematical categories, not merely higher positions on one ladder.

## 8. Common errors and epistemic boundaries

- **Membership is not hardness.** Short yes-certificates show NP membership, not that a problem is NP-complete.
- **The arrow must point correctly.** To prove B NP-hard, translate a *known NP-hard problem into B*.
- **Not every reduction is polynomial.** An exponential translator does not establish NP-hardness under the chosen reduction.
- **An example is not a proof for all instances.** The 3SAT-to-CLIQUE argument is universal because it proves both implications for an arbitrary 3CNF formula.
- **The source theorem is not being reproved.** Cook–Levin supplies SAT's initial hardness; our local gate and clique constructions carry that hardness forward.
- **Hardness is not membership.** Undecidable NP-hard languages need not lie in NP.

These distinctions keep the Atlas from conflating membership, hardness, completeness, decidability, and practical solvability.

## Map update

The Atlas now has a **hardness layer** distinct from its membership layer. Problems in NP admit efficiently checkable yes-certificates. A complete problem is a member of NP to which every NP language efficiently reduces. The 3SAT-to-CLIQUE construction makes this operational through a graph whose cliques encode mutually consistent choices of true literals.

The **strictness** of P versus NP is still unknown. The map must label $P\subseteq NP$ as proved, but $P\subsetneq NP$ as conjectural rather than established.
