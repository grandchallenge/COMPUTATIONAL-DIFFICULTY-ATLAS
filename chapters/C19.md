# C19 — Average-Case Complexity: Difficulty Under Which Distribution?

**Status:** Editorial candidate; review and figures pending.
**Prerequisites:** C02, C06, C14.

## Question: why can a worst-case difficult problem be routine in practice?

A solver may process thousands of structured instances quickly yet fail on a crafted input. Worst-case and typical performance quantify over different things.

## Section 1 — Distributional problems

A distributional decision problem is $(L,\mathcal D)$ with $D_n$ a distribution on length-$n$ inputs. For algorithm $A$,

$$
\mathbb E_{x\sim D_n} T_A(x)=\sum_{x\in\{0,1\}^n}D_n(x)T_A(x).
$$

A polynomial worst-case bound implies a polynomial expectation under every distribution; the converse fails for a fixed distribution.

## Section 2 — A rare expensive input

A deliberately inefficient correct decider for the all-zero language first reads its input, then waits $2^n$ steps on the all-zero string before accepting; otherwise it rejects. Under uniform bits the slow input has probability $2^{-n}$ and contributes exactly one expected extra waiting step in the abstract cost model. Expectation is linear while worst-case time is exponential. Concentrate the distribution on that input and expectation becomes exponential. This distinguishes measures of an algorithm, not intrinsic hardness of the language.

## Section 3 — Tail bounds and robust average time

Markov's inequality gives $\Pr(T>t)\le \mathbb E[T]/t$ for nonnegative $T$ and $t>0$. On the event $T>t$, the contribution to expectation is at least $t$ times its probability. A Levin-style polynomial-on-average condition instead requires $\mathbb E[T^\epsilon]\le C(n+1)^c$ for constants $\epsilon>0,C,c$, for a total correct algorithm and the specified distributions. A smaller positive moment can absorb a polynomial simulation overhead. This is not identical to a polynomial first moment.

## Section 4 — Distribution-preserving transfer

A worst-case reduction can send typical inputs to rare target inputs. For a length-preserving reduction $f$, a sufficient domination condition is

$$
\Pr_{x\sim D_n}[f(x)=y]\le p(n)E_n(y)
$$

for every target $y$ and polynomial $p$. Multiply by any nonnegative target cost and sum: expectation under the pushforward is at most $p(n)$ times target expectation. Positive moments follow likewise. More general reductions account for length changes. Ordinary NP-completeness alone does not establish distributional completeness.

## Section 5 — Cryptography and experiments

One-way functions ask for efficient forward evaluation and difficulty of inversion on generated inputs against specified adversaries. Worst-case NP-hardness alone does not establish their existence. Benchmarks must disclose instance generator, size, solver, timeout and censoring; empirical averages do not prove all-input complexity.
