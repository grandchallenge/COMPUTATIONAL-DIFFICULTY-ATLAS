## Question: is finding an answer fundamentally harder than checking one?

A crossword answer can be checked after it is filled in, but completing a difficult crossword may take much longer. The analogy captures an important distinction between construction and verification—but analogies do not establish complexity lower bounds.

The mathematical question is whether **every** decision problem whose yes-instances have polynomial-size, polynomial-time checkable witnesses also has a deterministic polynomial-time *decider*. That is the P versus NP problem.

We know how to prove that a given language belongs to P: design and analyze one decider. We know how to prove that a language belongs to NP: design a bounded verifier. We do **not** know whether these two conditions describe the same class.

## Section 1 — A clean statement of the problem

Fix a conventional binary encoding and deterministic Turing-machine cost model.

- $P$ contains languages decidable in deterministic time $n^{O(1)}$.
- $NP$ contains languages with polynomial-length yes-witnesses checked in deterministic time $n^{O(1)}$.

C04 proved the containment

$$
P\subseteq NP.
$$

The famous question asks whether it is strict:

$$
P\stackrel{?}{=}NP.
$$

It is currently **open**. Neither $P=NP$ nor $P\ne NP$ is an established theorem. The diagram of classes must therefore avoid depicting an unconditional gap between P and NP.

This is a worst-case theorem question about **decision languages**, not a claim about the typical difficulty of Sudoku, machine-learning training, or individual benchmark instances.

## Section 2 — SAT is a representative problem, not merely an example

C06 described Cook–Levin: SAT is NP-complete under deterministic polynomial-time many-one reductions.

Therefore

$$
SAT\in P
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
P=NP.
$$

**Forward direction.** If SAT belongs to P, then for each $L\in NP$, Cook–Levin provides a polynomial-time reduction $f$ with $x\in L$ exactly when $f(x)\in SAT$. Translating and then running the assumed SAT decider puts $L$ in P. Thus $NP\subseteq P$; combine this with the known reverse containment.

**Reverse direction.** If $P=NP$, then SAT belongs to P because SAT is in NP.

The same equivalence holds when SAT is replaced with any other NP-complete language under the same reduction notion. This is one reason finding a polynomial-time algorithm for even a single complete problem would be revolutionary.

The conditional result is rigorous. It does not prove that a polynomial-time SAT algorithm exists.

## Section 3 — How a hypothetical SAT decider could find an assignment

Suppose we are given a deterministic polynomial-time oracle-free decider $D$ for SAT. We can build a polynomial-time *search* algorithm that outputs a satisfying assignment whenever one exists.

On a well-formed formula $\varphi$ with variables explicitly represented in its encoding (at most its input bit length), the hypothetical **total** SAT decider first validates the input, rejecting malformed encodings. For a valid formula:

1. Ask $D(\varphi)$. If it says NO, report that the formula is unsatisfiable.
2. Choose an unset variable $x_1$. Ask whether the formula with $x_1=0$ is satisfiable. If yes, keep $x_1=0$; otherwise keep $x_1=1$.
3. Repeat for the remaining variables, each time keeping a value that preserves satisfiability.
4. Output the resulting assignment.

At most one extra SAT query is needed per variable, after the initial satisfiability query. There are at most $|\varphi|$ distinct variable occurrences/identifiers in any explicit encoding, and substituting Boolean constants and simplifying or re-encoding formulas takes polynomial bit time. Therefore, under the explicitly **hypothetical** polynomial-time SAT decider, the entire witness-recovery process is polynomial in $|\varphi|$.

**Correctness invariant.** After each choice, the partially assigned formula is still satisfiable. This holds initially by step 1, and at each stage at least one of the two possible values for the next variable preserves satisfiability. When no variables remain, the assignment makes the original formula true.

This is an example of **self-reducibility**. It explains why SAT's decision/search distinction does not obstruct a polynomial-time search algorithm *if* a polynomial-time SAT decider exists. It does **not** settle the hypothesis that one exists.

## Section 4 — The converse direction and lower bounds

To prove $P\ne NP$, it would suffice to prove that SAT is not in P, or equivalently that any one NP-complete language lacks a deterministic polynomial-time decider.

But the statement must hold against **every possible** deterministic polynomial-time algorithm, including future methods not resembling known solvers. Exhaustive search taking $2^m$ time in the number of variables is one upper bound for SAT, not a lower bound on all algorithms.

Unsuccessful algorithm searches, enormous empirical instances, and practical cryptographic assumptions are not unconditional complexity separations.

**Why is nonmembership so difficult?** An explicit algorithm demonstrates membership in an upper-bound class. Excluding every algorithm of an entire complexity class requires a general impossibility argument, which is often much harder.

## Section 5 — What time hierarchy theorems really tell us

Not every computational-resource separation is open. The deterministic time hierarchy theorem shows that, given sufficiently different time bounds and appropriate time-constructibility assumptions, extra time permits deciding additional languages.

One important consequence is the unconditional strict inclusion

$$
P\subsetneq EXPTIME,
$$

where $EXPTIME$ contains decision languages decidable in deterministic time $2^{n^{O(1)}}$.

This separation says there are **some** decidable problems for which no deterministic polynomial-time algorithm exists. But it does not identify SAT as one of them.

We also know the containments

$$
P\subseteq NP\subseteq PSPACE\subseteq EXPTIME.
$$

Because P and EXPTIME differ, at least one inclusion in this chain must be strict. That fact alone does **not** tell us which: it cannot be used to draw $P\subsetneq NP$, $NP\subsetneq PSPACE$, or $PSPACE\subsetneq EXPTIME$ as established.

The difference between "at least one of several inclusions is strict" and "this particular inclusion is strict" is one of the Atlas's essential visual rules.

## Section 6 — coNP and the double-verification question

C07 introduced coNP, the class of languages whose *no*-instances have short efficiently checkable witnesses.

We know

$$
P\subseteq NP\cap coNP.
$$

We do not know whether $NP=coNP$, nor whether $P=NP\cap coNP$. If the hypothetical equality $P=NP$ held, closure under complements would also force $P=NP=coNP$.

It would be wrong to conclude from the existence of separate SAT and TAUT certificates that NP and coNP have been proved different. Completeness results classify their relative hardness conditional on reductions; they do not supply the missing separation.

## Section 7 — Why apparently decisive proof strategies are not decisive

It is useful to know about obstacles discovered in past research, without mistaking them for impossibility theorems for every conceivable argument.

**Relativization.** Baker, Gill, and Solovay showed that there are oracle worlds with $P^A=NP^A$ and others with $P^B\ne NP^B$. A proof strategy that automatically remains valid under the same arbitrary oracle extension cannot resolve the unrelativized equality by itself. The result does not show that all diagonalization or simulation arguments are worthless.

**Natural proofs.** Razborov and Rudich established a conditional barrier for a broad class of circuit lower-bound methods under cryptographic pseudorandomness assumptions. It is not a theorem that no circuit lower-bound proof or no proof of P versus NP can exist.

**Algebrization.** Aaronson and Wigderson identified further limits of techniques surviving certain algebraic oracle extensions. This is a refinement of the methodology discussion, not a solution or disproof of P versus NP.

These barriers explain why attractive proof templates may fall short. They should be read with their precise hypotheses, not turned into universal claims that certain mathematical ideas are forbidden.

## Section 8 — If P equaled NP, what would follow?

Mathematically, many NP search tasks could be solved in deterministic polynomial time by reductions and witness reconstruction. SAT would have a polynomial-time decider; so would CLIQUE and other NP-complete decision languages.

The impact on deployed encryption or real-world computation would depend on the algorithm's degree, constants, and precise cryptographic setting. Polynomial time can still be impractical; some cryptographic assumptions concern average-case, distributional, or number-theoretic tasks not identical to worst-case NP-completeness.

Conversely, if $P\ne NP$, that would establish a worst-case separation for decision languages. It would not mean every instance of every NP-complete problem is difficult, nor rule out approximation, randomized success on particular distributions, preprocessing, or specialized algorithms.

Both hypothetical outcomes require careful interpretation.

## Section 9 — Common mistakes

- **"Checking is faster than solving, so P is not NP."** The informal observation is not a universal proof.
- **"An exponential SAT algorithm proves SAT needs exponential time."** A known upper bound is not an all-algorithms lower bound.
- **"P and NP are disjoint."** The proved relation is $P\subseteq NP$.
- **"P is distinct from EXPTIME, so P is distinct from NP."** The strict step in the containment chain has not been identified.
- **"A verifier automatically finds its witness."** Checking a supplied witness and finding one are distinct tasks; SAT self-reducibility works conditionally on a polynomial-time decision procedure.
- **"NP means nonpolynomial."** NP is defined by nondeterministic computation or polynomially bounded certificates, not by an assertion of superpolynomial runtime.
- **"A barrier theorem says the problem cannot be solved."** Barriers restrict specified proof techniques under specified assumptions, not every conceivable argument.

## Primary sources and theorem boundaries

Juris Hartmanis and Richard E. Stearns, *On the Computational Complexity of Algorithms* (1965), [DOI:10.1090/S0002-9947-1965-0170805-7](https://doi.org/10.1090/S0002-9947-1965-0170805-7), develops deterministic time-hierarchy methods underpinning the strict P-versus-EXPTIME separation. Theodore Baker, John Gill and Robert Solovay, *Relativizations of the P =? NP Question* (1975), [DOI:10.1137/0204037](https://doi.org/10.1137/0204037), gives oracle settings for both outcomes. Alexander A. Razborov and Steven Rudich, *Natural Proofs* (1997), [DOI:10.1006/jcss.1997.1494](https://doi.org/10.1006/jcss.1997.1494), establishes a conditional barrier to a specified class of constructive circuit-lower-bound arguments under strong pseudorandomness assumptions. Scott Aaronson and Avi Wigderson, *Algebrization: A New Barrier in Complexity Theory* (2009), [DOI:10.1145/1490270.1490272](https://doi.org/10.1145/1490270.1490272), identifies an additional model-sensitive proof-technique limitation. None of these papers proves $P\ne NP$ or $P=NP$.

## Map update

The complexity map now carries an explicit **knowledge-status axis**. The inclusion $P\subseteq NP$ is proved. The inequality $P\ne EXPTIME$ is proved. The equality or strictness of P versus NP remains unknown, as do the other particular adjacent separations in the standard containment chain.

The NP-complete problems provide *equivalent formulations* of the central decision question, but not a reason to draw the unknown boundary as a visible gap. Later chapters introduce quantified alternation, space and other resources; each addition must preserve the distinction between known containments and conjectural separations.
